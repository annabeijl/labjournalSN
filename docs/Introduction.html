Introduction

Anna Beijl

10 September 2026


1 Introduction


Intergroup contact theory is one of the most established theories in contemporary social science ((The Nature of Prejudice, n.d.); Pettigrew & Tropp). The central idea is that intergroup contact reduces intergroup prejudice; that is, if people have more contact with out-group members, they are more likely to develop positive attitudes on this out-group. “The contact hypothesis has stimulated an enormous body of research and has received support across a variety of settings and social groups (see Brown & Hewstone, 2005; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006).” “A critical issue that has long concerned contact researchers, however, is whether the effects of intergroup contact generalize beyond the specific contact experience to new situations, the entire outgroup, and other outgroups not directly involved in the contact (e.g., Amir, 1969, 1976; Ford, 1986; Hewstone & Brown, 1986; Pettigrew, 1997, 1998; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). These forms of generalization are crucial for the wider effectiveness and practical value of intergroup contact as an intervention to reduce prejudice.” Over time, the theoretical model has been extended to include all out-group members (bron), and even members of other out-groups that were not involved in the contact at all. The latter is known as a secondary transfer effect of intergroup contact (Pettigrew, 2009; Tausch et al., 2010), and has been studied extensively. 

The most important underlying mechanism of this transfer effect of intergroup contact seems to be attitude generalization, where contact or positive attitudes towards a primary outgroup form part of a broader (generalized) outgroup attitude (see also Allport, 1954) and in this way ‘spill over’ to other outgroups. In simpler terms, contact with one outgroup might make people realize that outgroups are not so bad at all, and make them more tolerant in general (cf. Tausch et al. 2010). However, the literature also describes a number of alternative explanations and theoreticl problems. For example, secondary transfer effects could also be explained by some respondents having more contact with (primary and secondary) outgroups in general (descibed as the secondary contact problem in Tausch et al., 2010); a social desirability problem (ibid); or a causal sequence problem where more tolerant repondents engage in more intergroup contact in general (ibid). The latter presents a big issue in particular, already raised by Pettigrew (2009) in his original text dubbing secondary transfer effects:

“Given this complex, bidirectional nature of the contact and prejudice relationship, we must focus on this problem: Do primary contact effects typically generalize to other, noncontacted out-groups? Or does the secondary transfer effect largely reflect the nonrecursive phenomenon whereby more tolerant people engage in more intergroup contact generally and harbor less prejudice against a wide variety of outgroups?” (Pettigrew, 2009, p. 56).

In other words, maybe this represents truly a selection effect where more tolerant people become friends with a range of out-group members. In order to examine this causal sequence, and test whether secondary transfer effects indeed reflect generalization / influence effects rather than selection bias, longitudinal and/or social network data is needed. [meer uitleg social networks perspectief]. “we need not only information on the social agents (e.g. people, organizations) but also on the ties (or relations) between them” (@SNASS). “A social network perspective will make explicit that empirical observations of individuals are not always independent. The complex (i.e. dynamic, multidimensional, non-linear) relations within social networks cause complex interdependencies between observations. This has consequences for many of our traditional research methods” To date, the social embeddedness and interdependence of people engaged in intergroup contact remains understudied (but see Bracegirdle et al. 2026; Zingora 2025; Zingora et al. 2026).


So: is this secondary transfer effect of intergroup contact actually because of attitude generalization, or could it actually (partially) reflect network dynamics, such as social influence? (cf. Zingora et al. 2026). Although this is very much an emerging field of study, multiple studies support the latter idea (Stark, 2019). Recent studies say: it’s actually fully influence, not transfer.

The secondary out-group friendship formation may depend on individual out-group attitudes, but may also depend on opportunity structures, existing relations, having friends in common, or structural network effects, such as reciprocity, transitivity, or clustering. To disentangle [xxx], social network data can form an important addition to the literature on secondary transfer effects of intergroup contact. “How social networks shape opportunities to form intergroup friendships, influence intergroup attitudes, and affect ongoing intergroup interactions and relationships. This emerging work reveals how friendships across group lines are shaped not only by the individuals involved but also by their other friends, the attitudes of those around them, and the structure and context of their broader social network” (Turetsky & Shelton, 2024).


Data and context justification. “We focused on interethnic friendships during adolescence because adolescents’ attitudes toward ethnic outgroups are more malleable than adults’ attitudes (Wölfer et al., 2016) and because adolescents are especially motivated to adopt the attitudes of significant peers and friends (Brechwald & Prinstein, 2011; Steinberg & Monahan, 2007).” Schools are shown to play an important role in stimulating positive contact between (ethnic) groups, both within and outside of the school environment (Karataş et al., 2023). → offering a secure environment with the facilitative conditions (see Allport; Pettigrew & Tropp). Can be transferred or ‘spilled over’ into other contexts

Based on the above literature and social network analysis, this paper will focus on the following research question on the macro level: “To what extent are students’ ethnic minority (secondary outgroup) attitudes clustered based on primary outgroup (gender) friendships?”. To further entangle this, using the general theoretical framework (cf. Coleman 1990), the micro level explanatory research question is: “To what extent can clustering in ethnic minority attitudes be explained by selection or influence effects?”. By bridging insights from intrgroup contact theory and social network analysis, this research offers a novel perspective on attitude change.

  • RQ1: What is the distribution of secondary out-group attitudes / friendships in the network?

  • RQ2: To what extent are secondary out-group attitudes / friendships explained by social influence effects (rather than secondary transfer effects of intergroup contact)?
