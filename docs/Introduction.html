Introduction

Anna Beijl

10 September 2026


1 Introduction


Intergroup contact theory is one of the most established theories in contemporary social science (Allport, 1954; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). The central idea is that intergroup contact reduces intergroup prejudice; that is, if people have more contact with outgroups, they are more likely to develop positive attitudes towards these outgroups. The so-called contact hypothesis has sparked an enormous body of research and has found support among many settings and social groups (see Brown & Hewstone, 2005; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). Intergroup contact is also often described as a promising and successful strategy or intervention to improve outgroup attitudes (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006; Zingora, 2025). Something that has been of particular interest to contact researchers, is whether the effects of intergroup contact generalize beyond the specific contact experience to new situations, the entire outgroup, and other outgroups not directly involved in the contact. The latter is known as a secondary transfer effect of intergroup contact (Pettigrew, 2009; Tausch et al., 2010), and constitutes the main focus of the current study. 

Secondary transfer effects (STEs) seem to revolve around attitude generalization, where contact or positive attitudes towards a primary outgroup form part of a broader (generalized) outgroup attitude (see also Allport, 1954) and in this way ‘spill over’ to other outgroups. In simpler terms, contact with one outgroup might make people realize that outgroups are not all that bad, and make them more tolerant in general (cf. Tausch et al. 2010). For example, contact with foreigners not only causes attitudes towards foreigners to improve, but also increases attitudes towards gay people (Pettigrew, 2009). However, the literature also describes a number of alternative explanations and theoretical problems. For example, it is possible that more tolerant people engage in more intergroup contact in general, reflecting a selection effect, rather than attitude generalization. This is a long-standing issue in STE research, already raised in the original text dubbing secondary transfer effects:

“Given this complex, bidirectional nature of the contact and prejudice relationship, we must focus on this problem: Do primary contact effects typically generalize to other, noncontacted out-groups? Or does the secondary transfer effect largely reflect the nonrecursive phenomenon whereby more tolerant people engage in more intergroup contact generally and harbor less prejudice against a wide variety of outgroups?” (Pettigrew, 2009, p. 56).

In other words, maybe this truly represents a selection effect, where more tolerant people become friends with a range of out-group members; complicating the evidence for classic STE theory. In order to examine this causal sequence, and to test whether secondary transfer effects indeed reflect generalization effects rather than selection bias, longitudinal and/or social network data is needed. Social network analysis is particularly well-suited for disentangling selection and influence effects, by taking into account the complex interdependencies between people (Tolsma & Hofstra, 2024). In order to accurately study the development of intergroup attitudes, we need not only data on individuals, but also on the social relations and dependencies between them, and how they affect each other. In other words: we not only need to examine the dots, but also (especially!) the arrows between them. To date, the social embeddedness and interdependence of people engaged in intergroup contact remains understudied (but see e.g. Bracegirdle et al. 2022; Wölfer et al., 2017; Zingora et al. 2026).

In addition to selection effects, another important factor to take into account are network dynamics. Recent research shows that outgroup attitudes are shaped not only by individual intergroup contact and attitudes, but also by other intervening network processes, such as social influence (Bracegirdle et al., 2022; Zingora, 2025; Zingora et al., 2026). Although secondary outgroup attitudes may indeed depend on individual (primary outgroup) attitudes or tolerance, they may also (partly) reflect structural network effects such as opportunity structures, existing relations, having friends in common, or the attitudes of those friends. The latter, which are better known as social influence processes (see also Flache, 2017), have until very recently, been neglected in the intergroup contact and STE literature. Although this is very much an emerging field of research, multiple studies find support for influence processes and suggest that network effects might actually be more prominent than secondary transfer or attitude generalization (Bracegirdle et al., 2022; Stark, 2019; Turetsky & Shelton, 2024; Zingora et al., 2026). Again, social network analysis can thus form an important addition to the literature on secondary transfer effects of intergroup contact.

Based on the above literature and the importance of an added social network perspective, this paper will focus on the following research question on the macro level: “To what extent are students’ social networks segregated based on primary or secondary outgroup attitudes?” (RQ1). To further disentangle this, using the general theoretical framework (cf. Coleman, 1990), the micro level explanatory research question is: “To what extent can students’ secondary outgroup attitudes be explained by selection and influence effects?” (RQ2). This paper offers a new perspective on STEs of intergroup contact, by bringing together insights from intergroup contact theory and social network analysis.

When studying these outgroup friendships and attitudes, schools are often used as an especially well-suited setting for various reasons (see Wölfer et al., 2017; Zingora, 2025). First, adolescents’ attitudes are seen as relatively malleable and susceptible to change from their friends and peers (Brechwald & Prinstein, 2011). Schools in particular often offer a secure environment that succesfully integrate the facilitative conditions for reducing intergroup prejudice (see Allport, 1954; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). Furthermore, the positive effects of intergroup contact in schools seem to spill over into other contexts outside the school environment (Karataş et al., 2023), further highlighting the importance of studying intergroup contact and attitudes among students. In this paper, I will therefore focus on students in the first years of secondary education in Nijmegen, the Netherlands, using TNSS network data (Tolsma & Pop, 2010). The primary and secondary outgroups in this study are based on gender and ethnicity, respectively.
