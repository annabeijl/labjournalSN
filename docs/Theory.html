Theory and Hypotheses

Anna Beijl

28 September 2026


1 Theory and Hypotheses


Intergroup contact theory has received a great deal of academic attention and has been extended a great deal since Allport’s (1954) seminal work. For example, growing bodies of research study indirect contact where an individual is not part of the interaction. Concepts like extended contact, that is, simply knowing an in-group member has out-group friends (Zhou et al., 2018) or vicarious contact; observing other in-group members (e.g. online or through reading stories, Tropp et al., 2022; Stark et al., 2025) have been shown to be successful in prejudice reduction. Experimental research even shows support for secondary transfer effects when contact with the primary group was merely observed in others or even imagined (Harwood et al., 2011; Vezzali et al., 2014). This highlights the wide reach and broad support for the supposed secondary transfer effect. However, research also shows that the STE seems to be conditional; the attitude generalization process likely only works when the primary and secondary outgroups are similar in culture, stigma, or status (see e.g. Harwood et al., 2011). This importance of the level of group similarity is also known as the generalization gradient effect (Pettigrew, 2009).

As described, the most important underlying mechanism of this STE of intergroup contact seems to be the generalization of attitudes from one outgroup to another (Pettigrew, 2009; Tausch et al., 2010). However, the literature also describes a number of alternative explanations and theoretical problems. For example, secondary transfer effects could also be explained by some respondents having more contact with (primary and secondary) outgroups in general (descibed as the secondary contact problem in Tausch et al., 2010); a social desirability problem (ibid); or a causal sequence problem where more tolerant repondents engage in more intergroup contact in general (ibid). Furthermore, secondary transfer effects are not always found for all groups and settings (see Zingora et al., 2026). Recent research proposes a social networks approach to tackle these problems in STE theory (Bracegirdle et al., 2021; Bracegirdle et al., 2026; Zingora, 2025; Zingora et al., 2026). The added value of a social networks perspective is already evident in other forms of contact research: for example, results from social network analysis show that extended contact reduces prejudice, only when some of the outgroup friends of one’s ingroup friends are known. So: only in mixed triads, not in open triads (Stark, 2019).


1.1 Selection effects

Another important network element lies, as described, in selection effects. To test the classic and individual-centered idea of attitude generalization, I first formulate H1. Continuing the selection idea, I build on the concept of homophily; the idea that ‘birds of a feather flock together’, or in other words: people prefer contact with others that are similar to them (McPherson et al., 2001). Based on Pettigrew’s (2009) original concern surrounding the tendency of tolerant people to befriend each other, I formulate the following hypotheses:

H1: Students with more positive primary outgroup (i.e. women) attitudes have, on average, more positive secondary outgroup (i.e. ethnic minority) attitudes as well.

H2: Students prefer to send ties to others with similar levels of primary outgroup (i.e. women) and secondary outgroup (i.e. ethnic minority) attitudes as them.


1.2 Influence effects

Adding to this, some recent studies suggest that social influence can help explain the lack of unequivocal support for STEs (Bracegirdle et al., 2026; Zingora et al., 2026). It might not only matter who someone is friends with, but also what those friends think about certain outgroups. The importance of social influence is further demonstrated by Bracegirdle et al. 2026 (p. 1), showing that “socialization was strongest among friends who held initially opposing attitudes” and thereby that “socialization has a neutralizing effect, rather than a polarizing effect”. Thus, it seems, friends’ out-group attitudes become more similar to each other over time. Another interesting complication lies in who influences who: is it all kinds of friends, or just in- or outgroup friends? And does it matter whether these friends are part of a (primary) outgroup themselves? Some studies suggest that social influence only works from ingroup and not outgroup friends (Bracegirdle et al., 2021), whilst other research highlight how the attitudes of ingroup members are often viewed as biased and therefore less persuasive (Zingora et al., 2026). Based on the literature, I exploratively expect:

H3: Students’ secondary outgroup (i.e. ethnic minority) attitudes are shaped by the average secondary outgroup attitudes of their friends.

H4: Students with more primary outgroup (i.e. based on gender) friends have, on average, more positive attitudes towards a secondary outgroup (i.e. ethnic minorities).


Based on both these selection and influence effects, I respect with regards to the macro research question:

H5: The TNSS network is segregated based on ethnic minority attitudes.
