Conclusion and Discussion

Anna Beijl

30 October 2026


1 Discussion


In this paper, I examined secondary transfer effects of intergroup contact among Dutch students using a social networks approach. Adding to a recent body of literature, the aim of this paper was to shed light on the interdependencies and peer influences in forming and changing attitudes. Specifically, I was interested in the transfer of intergroup attitudes from one outgroup to another. To model these primary and secondary outgroups, I looked at gender and ethnicity as forms of intergroup contact/attitudes in Dutch secondary education students (TNSS data, N = 28). Using social network analysis and RSiena models, I examined the research question: “To what extent are students’ social networks segregated based on primary (women) or secondary outgroup (ethnic minority) attitudes?”. To further explain this macro question on a micro/network level, I asked: “To what extent are ethnic minority attitudes explained by selection and influence effects?“ Based on a recent body literature that incorporates social network analysis and intergroup contact theory (see Bracegirdle et al., 2021; Zingora, 2025; Zingora et al., 2026) I expected that ethnic minority attitudes could be explained by primary outgroup friendships (i.e. based on gender). Additionally, I expected that secondary outgroup (i.e. ethnic minority) attitudes could be explained by attitudes that students - and especially their friends - hold towards a primary outgroup (i.e. women/girls).

In addition to theoretical innovation, this paper also used a short-term dynamic equilibrium SAOM approach (Krause et al., 2018; Snijders & Steglich, 2013), contributing to statistical innovation as well. Stationary SAOM analyses using both a network and a behavioral dependent variable have, to my knowledge, not yet been performed previously. The outcomes of this paper thus present some preliminary results (as well as challenges) on how to successfully combine these empirical approaches.


1.1 Conclusions and contributions

The results of descriptive analyses showed some level of segregation in the network based on ethnic minority attitudes, but only when giving lower weight to friends of friends. Interestingly, students do seem more alike to their direct neighbors in terms of ethnic minority attitudes, but this is not the case for classmates further apart from them. As became apparent through the visualizations, the classroom network was very clustered, but this was mostly based on gender rather than primary or secondary outgroup attitudes. Attitudes towards both women and ethnic minorities distributed with variation throughout the network, answering the first research question.

The RSiena models clearly highlighted the importance of incorporating the theoretical field of intergroup relations with a social networks perspective: it was not attitudes, but mostly structural network dynamics such as reciprocity and transitivity that played an important role in friendship formation. Further, the analyses showed indications for same-gender and same-ethnicity effects; students are likely to befriend others with the same gender (i.e. homophily), but are less likely to befriend others with the same ethnicity. This shows interethnic friendship in the sample was positively evaluated, which is especially interesting in light of the theoretical focus of this study. It could be argued that schools provide the four conditions that facilitate prejudice reduction, as described by Allport (1954) in his seminal work (see also Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006); equal status, common goals, intergroup cooperation, and support from authorities. This finding again highlights the important role of the classroom in stimulating positive interethnic interactions (Karataş et al., 2023; Zingora, 2025).

Looking back to the theoretical expectations, this paper found no support for secondary transfer effects from primary (gender) to secondary (ethnic minority) outgroups. Contrary to theoretical expectations, attitudes towards women did not have an effect on attitudes towards ethnic minorities in this sample. The same goes for predicting network outcomes based on these primary outgroup attitudes; neither the attitudes of students themselves, nor the level of similarity with others explained friendship formation. The primary outgroup attitudes of classmates, on the other hand, did seem to play a role; students are more likely to send a tie to others with more positive ethnic minority attitudes.

These contradictory findings could be explained by a difference in status or culture between the two outgroups. Previous literature describes the importance of group similarity for the generalization gradient effect (Harwood et al., 2011; Pettigrew, 2009). It could definitely be argued that women and ethnic minorities do not experience the same level of stigma and marginalization in the Netherlands - a relatively progressive and emancipated society in terms of gender, whilst at the same time characterized as quite exclusionary and discriminatory towards ethnic minorities (especially in recent years). Since this condition for the generalization gradient was likely violated, this could explain a lack of support for STE. Another explanation could lie in the outgroup construction and conditionality of the effects: maybe (and quite likely) this works differently for women and men, and for ethnic minority and majority members. This is also reflected in the importance of a student’s own ethnicity in predicting ethnic minority attitudes, as found in this study. Future research could further dive into this conditionality of outgroups by modelling interaction effects, or dyadic effects further specifying in- and outgroup membership and friendship.

Similar to primary outgroup attitudes, secondary outgroup attitudes did not predict network outcomes either in this sample. Relating back to the question posed by Pettigrew (2009) years ago, the analyses found no support for selection effects. It did not seem the case that students seek out their friendships on the basis of (primary or secondary) outgroup attitudes of their peers. There is, however, ample support for influence effects. Analyses on the behavioral model unequivocally showed that students adapt their ethnic minority attitudes to the average of their friends. This is in accordance with recent research on socialization and social influence in intergroup attitudes (Bracegirdle et al., 2021; Bracegirdle et al., 2026; Zingora et al., 2026).

As for the role of primary and secondary outgroup friends and their opinions on students’ secondary outgroup attitudes, the results of this paper are somewhat inconclusive. The main statistical effects specifying these specific influence processes, could not be included in the final model due to convergence issues. The question thus remains whether students with more primary outgroup (i.e. gender) friends tend to develop more positive attitudes towards a secondary outgroup (i.e. ethnic minorities). Similarly, based on my analyses, I also can not conclude whether students are especially likely to adopt an average secondary outgroup (i.e. ethnic minority) attitude of their primary outgroup (i.e. gender) friends. Recent research does point in this direction, suggesting that secondary outgroup attitudes change not due to having friends from the primary outgroup, but due to the attitudes that those primary outgroup friends hold toward secondary outgroups (Bracegirlde et al., 2026; Zingora et al., 2026).

Finally, the number of ethnic minority (i.e. secondary outgroup) friends did play an important role for secondary outgroup attitudes. Interestingly, though, a higher number of ethnic minority friends seemed to lead to a lower thermometer score and therefore more negative ethnic minority attitudes in this sample. This, again, could possibly be explained by the idea that it is not the mere friendships with these outgroup members that matters, but the outgroup attitudes of those friends. This is actually supported by recent research, that used simulation data (Zingora, 2025; Zingora et al., 2026). In simpler terms: if your (ingroup, primary outgroup, or secondary outgroup) friends hold negative attitudes towards an outgroup, social influence processes will cause you to be more negative towards this outgroup too. As strikingly put by Zingora and colleagues (2026, p. 446): “prior assumptions that outgroup friends are always associated with more positive secondary outgroup attitudes may have been overly optimistic.”


1.2 Limitations

First, as described in the data chapter, I made use of one-wave of network data. Although not explicitly criticized by the original authors of RSiena, this means that I analyzed cross-sectional data as if it was longitudinal network data. In doing so, I made assumptions about stability of the network (to be exact: that all network effects ‘cancel each other out’ which creates an almost identical network at T1 and T2), which might not reflect the actual network. Another limitation of the network data lies in the nominations; instead of friendship nominations by students themselves, this was done by mentors. This was purposefully chosen during data collection since one of the focal points of the TNSS data was bullying, which often presents itself more accurately by observed data. However, this could have further contributed to a biased picture of the social network; a network consisting of mostly reciprocated ties, with a couple of very popular/central students, and highly clustered friend groups that have almost no bridging ties between them.

Second, due to convergence issues, I suspect were most likely caused by the stationary SAOM approach, the full theorized model was not able to run. A model that includes all behavioral effects would have included my constructed dyadic covariate for outgroup friendships based on gender, interacted with alter effects (as described in more detail in the chapter RSiena results). However, after repeated trials, I was not able to succesfully run a model that converged. I have therefore not included the interpretations of this model in this paper, as the estimates are not to be trusted. Also, I could not run a model that included all network effects and all behavioral effects. Again, such models did not converge (even after following the stepwise cumulative model building approach as suggested by Ripley et al., 2026), which means that I can only draw conclusions about both models separately, but not controlled for each other. In terms of combining the stationary SAOM approach with a behavioral outcome, it proved important to also fix the behavioral rate parameter too a ‘high but not too high value’ (cf. Snijders & Steglich, 2013). Additionally, it seems like complex higher-order effects (such as interactions) are too complicated for this kind of model - at least in my case.


In conclusion, this paper presents both theoretical and methodological innovations regarding the combination of intergroup contact theory and social network analysis. Using a short-term dynamic equilibrium approach, I uncovered that secondary outgroup attitudes are best explained by social influence, rather than selection effects or attitude generalization (Pettigrew, 2009; Tausch et al., 2010). This conclusion clearly underscores the importance of a social networks perspective; none of these interdependencies and interpersonal complexities could have been revealed with traditional (i.e. regression-based) methods. In accordance with the most recent literature (Zingora et al., 2026), it seems that it is indeed not individual tolerance, nor friendships themselves that explain intergroup attitudes - instead what matters is the attitudes of those friends.
